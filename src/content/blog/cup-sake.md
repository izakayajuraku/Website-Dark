One of the most underrated corners of Japanese drinking culture is cup sake (カップ酒).

Once associated with train station convenience stores and casual drinking, cup sake has evolved into a highly respected and collectible category of premium sake.

## The History of Cup Sake

Cup sake was first introduced in 1964 by Ozeki Sake to cater to visitors attending the Tokyo Olympics.

The idea was simple: a single-serving, ready-to-drink sake that required no additional cup or preparation. It was an instant success, especially among travelers, businessmen, and workers who appreciated its convenience, affordability, and portability.

Over time, cup sake became a staple in Japanese convenience stores, train station kiosks, and vending machines. While it started out as inexpensive, everyday sake, many premium breweries have since embraced the format, now offering high-quality craft sake in cup form.

## Why Cup Sake Is Popular in Japan

- Convenience: no need for a sake set, just open and drink
- Perfect portion size: typically 180ml (1 go), ideal for a single serving
- Collectible designs: many producers decorate their cups with beautiful artwork, local landmarks, or traditional patterns
- Versatility: available in a variety of styles, from junmai and honjozo to daiginjo and nigori

## How Cup Sake Is Enjoyed in Japan

- On trains and travel: a favorite for shinkansen trips, enjoyed with ekiben (train station bento boxes)
- At festivals and outdoor events: cup sake stalls are common at Japanese festivals
- At home and casual drinking: perfect for when you don't want to open a full bottle
- In izakayas and bars: some serve cup sake as a nostalgic or premium offering
